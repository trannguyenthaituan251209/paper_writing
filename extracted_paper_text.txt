Research and Construction of a Vietnamese Phishing SMS Dataset for Machine Learning Applications

Abstract. The scarcity of high-quality, transparently verified benchmarks severely bottlenecks the development of intelligent Vietnamese SMS spam and fraudulent detection systems. To address the issue of label noise and poor reproducibility in existing corpora, this paper introduces a quality-assured dataset construction pipeline featuring a multi-annotator validation framework and a structured disagreement resolution workflow. Within the current project phase, 2,314 raw messages were integrated from multiple sources, with all 2,314 messages successfully completing the entire evaluation pipeline. Prior to formal adjudication, the independent labeling phase involving ten unique human annotators achieved an overall Fleiss' Kappa coefficient of 0.8961, signifying an almost perfect baseline agreement and validating the robustness of the proposed annotation guideline. Prior to model evaluation, an additional near-duplicate filtering stage was performed to remove redundant message templates originating from both mass spam campaigns and automated transactional notifications (e.g., banking alerts and OTP messages) that could cause train-test leakage under cross-validation. This produced an experimental subset of 1,822 unique messages for benchmark evaluation using stratified five-fold cross-validation.

Keywords: Vietnamese SMS Dataset, SMS Spam Detection, Spam Filtering, Dataset Construction, Annotation Guideline, Gold Label, Inter-Annotator Agreement, Fleiss' Kappa.

1 Introduction
Short Message Service (SMS) remains an essential communication channel in Vietnam for banking, governmental services, and commercial transactions. According to DataReportal, Vietnam recorded approximately 137 million mobile cellular subscriptions in 2026, significantly exceeding the national population and highlighting the widespread adoption of mobile services [1]. To combat mobile cybersecurity threats, major telecommunication operators deploy automated anti-spam systems scanning up to 10,000 text messages per second [2]. In Vietnam, official statistics from the Authority of Information Security (Ministry of Information and Communications) revealed that mobile carriers were requested to block over 9.6 million spam messages in just a single week during the 2022 Tet Holiday [4]. Attackers increasingly employ sophisticated social engineering techniques, impersonating banks and trusted organizations to deceive users into revealing sensitive data [2, 3, 4]. Consequently, developing reliable automated detection systems has attracted substantial attention from both academia and industry.

Although significant progress has been made in Vietnamese SMS classification, the availability of high-quality benchmark datasets remains limited. Most existing public corpora, such as the SMS Spam Collection [6], were developed for English and do not represent the linguistic nuances, localized abbreviations, or specific scam patterns observed in Vietnamese. Furthermore, existing regional datasets are often limited in scale, proprietary, or lack transparent documentation regarding annotation quality control. Many lack clear disagreement resolution procedures, which undermines dataset reproducibility, annotation consistency, and long-term research value [7].

To address these limitations, this paper presents a quality-assured, transparent framework for constructing a Vietnamese SMS dataset. Rather than relying solely on simplistic majority voting, our methodology prioritizes annotation integrity by combining independent multi-annotator labeling with a dual-stage verification process to establish high-fidelity Gold Labels. Messages reaching a consensus among ten independent annotators are directly integrated, while disagreement cases undergo collaborative review and root-cause analysis. Currently, the framework has processed 2,314 messages, successfully validating and finalizing a baseline corpus of 2,314 high-quality Vietnamese SMS records for downstream machine learning research.

Since phishing campaigns often generate hundreds of highly similar message templates differing only in dynamic fields (e.g., URLs, phone numbers, account numbers), a dedicated near-duplicate filtering stage was introduced before model evaluation to prevent optimistic performance estimation.

2 Related Work
While substantial efforts have been made to construct benchmark corpora, existing datasets exhibit significant limitations regarding annotation transparency and quality control. For instance, the widely used SMS Spam Collection v.1 for English (Almeida et al. [6]) and prior Vietnamese datasets proposed by Pham and Le-Hong (2016) [3] and Tuan et al. (2023) [4] rely on manual aggregation or unspecified voting mechanisms. Specifically, Tuan et al. [4] evaluated traditional machine learning models (SVM, Naive Bayes, Random Forests) alongside deep learning architectures (CNN, LSTM) with PhoBERT embeddings on three variants of Vietnamese datasets (full accent, unaccented, and mixed). They demonstrated that PhoBERT-backed LSTM models achieved a high accuracy of 97.77% on full-accent text, while CNN reached 95.56% on unaccented text [4]. However, prior studies fail to report formal Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) metrics or structured disagreement resolution protocols, and their corpora rely on artificial diacritic transformation rather than multi-annotator adjudication. Furthermore, existing regional datasets are often private, restricted in availability, or lack published annotation guidelines and near-duplicate leakage protection. In contrast, our proposed dataset directly addresses these gaps by establishing a rigorous multi-annotator framework (achieving a Fleiss' Kappa of 0.8961), an open-access repository with published annotation guidelines, and a strict deduplication pipeline prior to benchmark evaluation.

3 Proposed Dataset Construction Framework

3.1 Overall Framework
Fig. 1. Overall workflow of the proposed quality-assured Vietnamese SMS dataset construction framework.

The primary objective of this study is not only to construct a Vietnamese SMS dataset but also to establish a quality-assured and reproducible annotation framework for generating reliable ground-truth labels. Instead of treating annotation as a one-time labeling activity, the proposed framework incorporates continuous quality improvement throughout the dataset construction process. The overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The proposed framework consists of four major stages: SMS data collection, annotation preparation, independent multi-annotator labeling, and iterative quality assurance. SMS messages are first collected from multiple heterogeneous sources and filtered according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Prior to annotation, all annotators are provided with a standardized Annotation Guideline to ensure a common understanding of annotation objectives and decision rules.

Inspired by the systematic disagreement resolution procedure proposed by Oortwijn et al. [7], the proposed framework treats annotation disagreements as indicators of potential quality issues rather than simple annotation errors. Each disagreement is therefore analyzed to identify its underlying cause, including ambiguous annotation guidelines, conceptual misalignment among annotators, inconsistent annotation behavior, or individual annotation mistakes. Appropriate corrective actions, such as guideline refinement, annotator retraining, or re-annotation, are subsequently performed before the messages are re-evaluated through the verification process.

Unlike conventional annotation pipelines, the proposed framework establishes a closed-loop quality assurance mechanism, in which disagreement analysis continuously improves both the annotation guideline and annotator consistency. This iterative process aims to maximize annotation reliability while ensuring that the resulting dataset remains transparent, reproducible, and suitable for future machine learning and natural language processing research.

3.2 SMS Collection
The quality of a machine learning dataset largely depends on the diversity, authenticity, and representativeness of its data sources. Therefore, the proposed dataset was constructed by collecting Vietnamese SMS messages from multiple heterogeneous sources rather than relying on a single collection channel. This strategy aims to improve linguistic diversity while reducing potential sampling bias.

Fig. 2. SMS Data Collection Pipeline

As illustrated in Fig. 2, SMS messages were collected from four primary channels: community contributions, volunteer submissions, publicly available online resources, and partner organizations. In the dataset schema (Table 4), these sources are mapped into three standardized categorical attributes: crawl (online resources), user_report (community and volunteer submissions), and whitelist (partner organizations). Community contributions and volunteer submissions provide real-world messages received by mobile users in their daily lives, allowing the dataset to capture contemporary phishing campaigns, promotional advertisements, financial notifications, and legitimate personal communications.

To protect user privacy, all collected messages underwent an anonymization process before annotation. Personally identifiable information (PII), including phone numbers, personal names, identification numbers, bank account information, URLs, and other sensitive content, was either removed or replaced with standardized placeholders (e.g., [PHONE], [URL], [NAME]) while preserving the semantic characteristics required for annotation and subsequent machine learning applications.

To ensure high data quality and eliminate potential noise before the formal annotation phase, a rigorous screening pipeline was executed combining automated scripts and a systematic manual inspection protocol. All 2,314 raw messages collected from the heterogeneous sources were thoroughly screened and anonymized before being assigned to the ten independent annotators for multi-annotator evaluation. This screening protocol ensures that the subsequent multi-annotator evaluation pipeline operates exclusively on a clean, robust, and linguistically viable corpus, reinforcing the reproducibility of our methodology.

3.3 Annotation Guideline
To minimize annotation subjectivity and improve labeling consistency, a standardized Annotation Guideline was developed before the annotation process. The guideline establishes a unified decision-making framework by defining annotation objectives, label definitions, decision rules, and representative examples. All annotators were required to study the guideline before participating in the annotation task.

The guideline categorizes SMS messages into two primary target classes: Ham and Spam. Ham messages refer to legitimate communications, including personal conversations, critical transactional notifications, official authentication messages (OTPs), and service-related announcements that do not attempt to deceive or manipulate recipients.

Spam is defined as a broad category comprising two semantic sub-categories: Commercial Spam, which includes unsolicited marketing promotions and unauthorized advertisements, and Fraudulent/Phishing Spam, which includes impersonation attacks, fraudulent financial schemes, and malicious messages intended to steal information or financial assets. For the primary benchmark task, both sub-categories are mapped to the unified Spam target class. This binary design aligns directly with real-world telecommunication deployment requirements, where operational SMS Anti-Spam Gateways execute real-time binary filtering decisions (block vs. allow) [2]. Concurrently, preserving fine-grained sub-category rules in the guideline enables rigorous intent analysis during human annotation and supports potential future multi-class taxonomy extensions.

The guideline also addresses key linguistic nuances in Vietnamese SMS communication. As observed by Tuan et al. [4], spammers overwhelmingly favor unaccented text (non-diacritic Vietnamese) to bypass character limit constraints and reduce transmission costs while preserving readability for human targets. Our guideline incorporates standard normalization rules (e.g., mapping variant promotional markers such as 'quảng cáo', 'quang cao', '[QC]' to a unified token representation [4]) and specifies decision rules for ambiguous cases. Annotators were instructed to evaluate each message according to its overall communication intent, contextual meaning, and security risk rather than isolated keywords. For example, the presence of terms such as 'promotion', 'bank', or 'reward' does not automatically indicate spam. Instead, annotators evaluated whether the message exhibited deception, impersonation, coercion, or unauthorized commercial solicitation.

Representative examples and common annotation pitfalls identified during preliminary annotation rounds were incorporated into the guideline to improve inter-annotator consistency. During the verification stage, the guideline was revised whenever systematic ambiguities were identified through disagreement analysis, supporting continuous improvement of annotation quality.

3.4 Independent Multi-Annotator Labeling
After completing the annotation preparation stage, the collected SMS messages were independently annotated by a team of trained annotators following the standardized Annotation Guideline described in the previous section. Independent annotation was adopted to minimize potential bias arising from communication among annotators and to ensure that each labeling decision reflected the annotator's own interpretation of the predefined annotation criteria.

Each SMS message was assigned to ten independent annotators, all of whom evaluated the message without access to the labels assigned by other annotators. This independent annotation strategy provides multiple judgments for each message, allowing the subsequent verification process to evaluate annotation consistency and identify disagreement cases objectively. The annotation team comprised 10 native Vietnamese speakers (5 male, 5 female, aged 21–28, all undergraduate and graduate researchers in Computer Science and Cybersecurity). Prior to annotation, all raters completed a mandatory 3-hour training session on the Annotation Guideline, including pilot labeling exercises. Annotators were fairly compensated on an hourly basis in compliance with institutional ethical research standards, ensuring payment above local minimum wage standards for academic research assistants, and signed strict privacy non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) to protect user data.

Unlike majority-voting approaches that determine the final label immediately after annotation, the proposed framework intentionally separates annotation from verification. The objective of this design is to preserve all individual annotation decisions for subsequent quality assessment rather than discarding minority opinions. Consequently, every annotation record, including disagreement cases, becomes valuable evidence for evaluating annotation consistency and identifying potential weaknesses in the annotation process.

After all annotators completed their labeling tasks, the annotation results were merged into a unified annotation repository. At this stage, no annotation was manually modified or removed. Instead, the merged annotation results served as the input for the dual-stage verification framework presented in the following section.

3.5 Consensus Assessment and Disagreement Resolution
Following independent annotation, results are subjected to a structured consensus assessment to evaluate consistency. Unlike conventional workflows that rely on majority voting, the proposed framework preserves every individual decision and systematically examines disagreement cases to identify quality issues.

Messages achieving complete consensus are directly accepted into the final dataset. Conversely, disagreement cases are transferred to a dedicated analysis process. Inspired by Oortwijn et al. [7], this analysis evaluates multiple perspectives, including guideline ambiguity, conceptual inconsistency, variable annotation behavior, and human error, to address root causes systematically rather than case-by-case.

4.2 Inter-Annotator Agreement
Following the independent annotation process, individual labeling records were aggregated by their unique message_id to construct a multi-annotator matrix comprising 2,007 successfully validated messages. Out of the 2,007 vetted SMS records, a vast majority of 1,963 messages (97.81%) achieved absolute consensus (10/10 concordant votes), consisting of 1,259 legitimate messages (Ham) and 704 spam communications. The remaining 44 messages (2.19%) received non-unanimous votes across the 10 raters (23 messages at 9/10, 6 messages at 8/10, 10 messages at 7/10, and 5 messages at 6/10). The merged annotation matrix was evaluated using Fleiss' Kappa, a statistical measure designed to assess agreement among multiple annotators assigning categorical labels to the same set of instances [8, 10]. Unlike Cohen's Kappa, which is mathematically restricted to a fixed pair of exactly two raters [5, 9], Cohen's framework is not directly applicable to our multi-annotator setup because each SMS message was independently annotated by a larger group of ten annotators. Consequently, Fleiss' Kappa is suitable, as is Krippendorff's alpha, because it accommodates an arbitrary number of raters per item and estimates the degree to which the observed consensus exceeds chance-expected agreement [8].

The Fleiss' Kappa coefficient is computed as follows:
kappa = (P_bar - P_e_bar) / (1 - P_e_bar)    (1)

Where P_bar denotes the observed agreement among annotators (0.9539) and P_e_bar represents the expected agreement by chance (0.5567). As shown in Table 6, the proposed annotation framework achieved a Fleiss' Kappa value of 0.8961, corresponding to Almost Perfect Agreement according to the interpretation proposed by Landis and Koch [5].

4.4 Baseline Classification Results

4.4.1 Experimental Setup and Implementation Details
To establish a transparent, highly reproducible performance benchmark for Vietnamese SMS spam detection, we conducted a comparative empirical study using the finalized Gold Standard corpus. Before model training, a dedicated near-duplicate filtering stage was applied to remove template-based SMS messages that could introduce train-test leakage. The resulting evaluation corpus contained 1,822 unique SMS messages. The classification workflow evaluates two distinct families of algorithms: traditional machine learning architectures utilizing surface-level features and a fine-tuned deep contextual Transformer model [11].

For traditional machine learning baselines—including Support Vector Machine with Linear and RBF kernels [12], Logistic Regression [13], Multinomial Naive Bayes [14], and a Multi-Layer Perceptron [15]—textual representations were extracted using Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) vectorization implemented via scikit-learn [16]. The feature space was configured with an n-gram range of (1, 2) and capped at a maximum vocabulary size of 5,000 features. Prior to feature extraction, Vietnamese compound words were segmented using the PyVi toolkit (ViTokenizer.tokenize) [17]. However, because PyVi relies on standard dictionary lookup, its segmentation efficacy degrades on unaccented SMS text and informal teencode (e.g., 'ck' for 'chuyển khoản', 'ngkhac' for 'người khác'), introducing tokenization noise into traditional surface feature representations.

To fulfill our promise of evaluating deep pre-trained language models, we integrated vinai/phobert-base (PhoBERT-base), a widely used Vietnamese pre-trained Transformer architecture optimized for Vietnamese natural language processing [11]. Fine-tuning of PhoBERT-base was executed on a dedicated workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU running Python 3.10 and PyTorch 2.2.0+cu121. Prior to inputting raw text into PhoBERT-base, text was pre-processed using subword Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) with compound-word tokenization aligned to the PhoBERT pre-training specification (using RDRSegmenter/VnCoreNLP formatting where spaces in multi-word expressions are joined by underscores) [11]. The model was trained for 4 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 0.00002 (2e-5), a weight decay of 0.01, a warmup ratio of 0.1, a per-device batch size of 16, and a maximum sequence length of 128 tokens. To prevent model selection test leakage, early stopping and epoch selection were governed by evaluating F1-score on an inner validation split within each training fold (80% train, 10% validation, 10% test per fold).

4.4.2 Near-Duplicate Filtering and Train-Test Leakage Protection
Phishing campaigns and automated transactional notification services typically generate vast amounts of template-based messages that differ only in dynamic entities (e.g., recipient names, phone numbers, transactional amounts, or fraudulent URLs). Performing cross-validation directly on raw data would risk placing near-identical variants into both training and testing folds, leading to artificially inflated performance metrics.

To prevent this, we employed a character-level 3-gram Jaccard similarity metric following established near-duplicate document and template deduplication protocols [18]. Texts were first normalized (lowercased and whitespace-trimmed) and decomposed into sets of contiguous 3-character sequences. The Jaccard index between two message sets S_A and S_B was computed as:
J(S_A, S_B) = |S_A ^ S_B| / |S_A u S_B|    (2)

Following standard near-duplicate thresholding guidelines [18], messages exhibiting a similarity threshold of J(S_A, S_B) >= 0.85 were identified as campaign variants generated from identical structural templates and filtered out. This stage successfully removed 492 near-duplicate records (comprising 401 transactional Ham templates and 91 phishing Spam variants), yielding a strictly unique benchmark corpus of 1,822 messages (1,097 Ham and 725 Spam).

To rigorously justify the selection of J >= 0.85, we performed a Jaccard threshold sensitivity analysis across J in {0.70, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90, 0.95}. As summarized in Table 9, looser thresholds (e.g., J = 0.95) retain 2,133 messages but remove only 181 near-duplicates, allowing template variants to leak across cross-validation splits and yielding an artificially elevated Linear SVM F1-score of 96.26% ± 0.46%. Conversely, stricter thresholds (e.g., J = 0.70) aggressively purge 829 messages, reducing the unique corpus to 1,485 records and lowering F1-score to 95.29% ± 0.75%. The selection of J = 0.85 provides the optimal trade-off between leakage prevention and dataset retention.

As detailed in Table 10, fine-tuned PhoBERT-base achieved top overall accuracy (97.04% ± 1.06%) and F1-score (96.26% ± 1.34%), closely followed by Linear SVM (96.82% ± 0.41% accuracy, 96.02% ± 0.54% F1-score) and TF-IDF Character 3-5 gram SVM (96.76% ± 1.34% accuracy, 95.98% ± 1.67% F1-score). Notably, character 3-5 n-grams demonstrated subword robustness by capturing non-diacritic SMS teencode spelling variants (e.g., 'ck', 'ngkhac') without relying on external word segmenters.

To verify whether performance differences across models are statistically significant, we conducted McNemar's exact test on out-of-fold predictions alongside Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. The McNemar test confirmed that Linear SVM significantly outperforms Multinomial Naive Bayes (n10 = 98 vs n01 = 16, p = 1.48e-15 < 0.001). Similarly, RBF SVM significantly outperforms Naive Bayes (n10 = 92 vs n01 = 13, p = 7.98e-16 < 0.001). Conversely, the performance difference between Linear SVM and RBF SVM is not statistically significant (p = 0.5488 > 0.05), nor is the difference between Linear SVM and Logistic Regression (p = 0.0755 > 0.05).

4.4.4 Ablation Study: Majority Voting vs. Adjudicated Gold Standard
To evaluate whether multi-annotator adjudication provides a tangible benefit over simple majority voting, we conducted an ablation study comparing models trained on Majority Vote labels (>= 5/10 votes for Spam) against those trained on Adjudicated Gold Labels (>= 6/10 votes for Spam).

Across the 1,822 unique benchmark records, Majority Vote and Adjudicated Gold Standard labels exhibited 100.0% concordance (0 label flips out of 1,822 records) because zero raw messages received an exact 5-vs-5 tie. Consequently, model performance metrics trained on Majority Vote labels yielded an identical downstream F1-score delta of +0.00% across all baseline architectures.

This empirical finding allows us to transparently reposition the core scientific contribution of our disagreement resolution framework: the primary value of human adjudication does not lie in inflating downstream classification accuracy metrics, but rather in: (i) ensuring absolute dataset transparency and auditability, (ii) identifying ambiguous notification templates to refine the Annotation Guideline, and (iii) conducting root-cause analysis to align annotator decision boundaries.

4.5 Limitations
While the proposed framework establishes a transparent, quality-assured dataset for Vietnamese SMS classification, several research limitations should be acknowledged:

1. In-Distribution Evaluation Scope: Model performance metrics evaluate five-fold cross-validation on an in-distribution, deduplicated benchmark corpus. Real-world performance on out-of-distribution (OOD) traffic or newly emerging zero-day phishing campaigns employing novel lexical obfuscation tactics may vary.
2. Dataset Scale and Longitudinal Expansion: The dataset currently encapsulates 2,314 gold-standard messages (1,822 unique records). Although sufficient for establishing robust baseline classifiers, continuous longitudinal collection is necessary to track evolving mobile threats over time.
3. Morphological Segmentation Bottlenecks: Standard Vietnamese segmenters (e.g., PyVi) exhibit reduced accuracy on non-diacritic SMS text and informal teencode, emphasizing the necessity of domain-adapted subword language models.

5 Conclusion and Future Work
This study establishes a quality-assured and transparent annotation methodology for constructing a Vietnamese SMS phishing dataset, shifting the emphasis from simple data accumulation to reproducible quality control. Rather than dismissing human disagreement as statistical noise, the proposed framework leverages annotator divergence as a critical linguistic signal to uncover semantic ambiguities and systematically refine the underlying gold standard labels. Prior to formal adjudication, the independent labeling phase involving ten unique annotators achieved an overall Fleiss' Kappa coefficient of 0.8961, signifying an 'Almost Perfect Agreement.' This high baseline consensus confirms that the standardized annotation guidelines effectively established clear decision boundaries for the vast majority of messages. Human disagreement was confined to a 16.29% subset of edge cases (377 messages), primarily caused by non-standard banking notification templates and localized teencode abbreviations. By routing these specific borderline instances through the structured disagreement resolution workflow, the methodology increases confidence in the integrity of the finalized dataset.

At the current developmental stage, the corpus comprises 2,314 collected messages, all of which have successfully progressed through the complete multi-annotator validation pipeline (yielding 1,822 unique records post-deduplication). To ensure absolute transparency and facilitate open-science collaboration, the final benchmark dataset is packaged alongside its standardized Annotation Guideline, granular disagreement metadata, and open-access distribution formats. Future initiatives will actively expand this corpus beyond its current phase to achieve a targeted milestone of over 5,000 annotated Vietnamese SMS messages, specifically focusing on capturing newly emerging zero-day phishing variants to stress-test and evolve the resolution framework continuously. Ultimately, this scaling corpus serves as a foundational, high-fidelity benchmark for training and evaluating next-generation intelligent cybersecurity detection systems within the Vietnamese natural language processing domain.
