[1] Research and Construction of a Vietnamese Spam SMS Dataset for Machine Learning Applications
[2] Abstract. The scarcity of high-quality, transparently verified benchmarks severely bottlenecks the development of intelligent Vietnamese SMS spam and fraudulent detection systems. To address the issue of label noise and poor reproducibility in existing corpora, this paper introduces a quality-assured dataset construction pipeline featuring a multi-annotator validation framework and a structured disagreement resolution workflow. Within the current project phase, 2,058 raw messages were integrated from multiple sources, with a subset of 2,007 messages successfully completing the entire evaluation pipeline. Prior to formal adjudication, the independent labeling phase involving ten unique human annotators achieved an overall Fleiss' Kappa coefficient of 0.9856, signifying an almost perfect baseline agreement and validating the robustness of the proposed annotation guideline. Prior to model evaluation, an additional near-duplicate filtering stage was performed to remove redundant message templates originating from both mass spam campaigns and automated transactional notifications (e.g., banking alerts and OTP messages) that could cause train-test leakage under cross-validation. This produced an experimental subset of 1,541 unique messages for benchmark evaluation using stratified five-fold cross-validation.
[3] Keywords: Vietnamese SMS Dataset, SMS Spam Detection, Spam Filtering, Dataset Construction, Annotation Guideline, Gold Label, Inter-Annotator Agreement, Fleiss' Kappa.
[4] Introduction
[5] Short Message Service (SMS) remains an essential communication channel in Vietnam for banking, governmental services, and commercial transactions. According to DataReportal, Vietnam recorded approximately 137 million mobile cellular subscriptions in 2026, significantly exceeding the national population and highlighting the widespread adoption of mobile services [1]. However, this ubiquity has led to a persistent rise in SMS spam, creating major cybersecurity challenges. Attackers increasingly employ sophisticated social engineering techniques, impersonating banks and trusted organizations to deceive users into revealing sensitive data [3, 4]. Consequently, developing reliable automated detection systems has attracted substantial attention from both academia and industry.
[6] Although significant progress has been made in Vietnamese SMS classification, the availability of high-quality benchmark datasets remains limited. Most existing public corpora, such as the SMS Spam Collection [6], were developed for English and do not represent the linguistic nuances, localized abbreviations, or specific scam patterns observed in Vietnamese. Furthermore, existing regional datasets are often limited in scale, proprietary, or lack transparent documentation regarding annotation quality control. Many lack clear disagreement resolution procedures, which undermines dataset reproducibility, annotation consistency, and long-term research value [7].
[7] 1.1 Related Work and Existing Vietnamese SMS Corpora
[8] While SMS spam detection has been extensively studied for English corpora such as the SMS Spam Collection [6], research on Vietnamese SMS corpora remains limited. Prior works by Pham and Le-Hong [3] and Tuan et al. [4] introduced initial datasets for Vietnamese spam filtering using traditional machine learning algorithms. However, existing regional datasets present key limitations: (i) restricted public availability or small sample scale, (ii) absence of published, standardized annotation guidelines, (iii) lack of reported Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) metrics such as Fleiss' Kappa, and (iv) reliance on simple majority voting without formal disagreement resolution. [36]  Proposed Dataset Construction Framework
[37] Overall Framework
[38] Fig. 1. Overall workflow of the proposed quality-assured Vietnamese SMS dataset construction framework.
[39] The primary objective of this study is not only to construct a Vietnamese SMS dataset but also to establish a quality-assured and reproducible annotation framework for generating reliable ground-truth labels. Instead of treating annotation as a one-time labeling activity, the proposed framework incorporates continuous quality improvement throughout the dataset construction process. The overall workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.
[40] The proposed framework consists of four major stages: SMS data collection, annotation preparation, independent multi-annotator labeling, and iterative quality assurance. SMS messages are first collected from multiple heterogeneous sources and filtered according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Prior to annotation, all annotators are provided with a standardized Annotation Guideline to ensure a common understanding of annotation objectives and decision rules.
[41] Inspired by the systematic disagreement resolution procedure proposed by Oortwijn et al. [7], the proposed framework treats annotation disagreements as indicators of potential quality issues rather than simple annotation errors. Each disagreement is therefore analyzed to identify its underlying cause, including ambiguous annotation guidelines, conceptual misalignment among annotators, inconsistent annotation behavior, or individual annotation mistakes. Appropriate corrective actions, such as guideline refinement, annotator retraining, or re-annotation, are subsequently performed before the messages are re-evaluated through the verification process.
[42] Unlike conventional annotation pipelines, the proposed framework establishes a closed-loop quality assurance mechanism, in which disagreement analysis continuously improves both the annotation guideline and annotator consistency. This iterative process aims to maximize annotation reliability while ensuring that the resulting dataset remains transparent, reproducible, and suitable for future machine learning and natural language processing research.
[43] SMS Collection
[44] The quality of a machine learning dataset largely depends on the diversity, authenticity, and representativeness of its data sources. Therefore, the proposed dataset was constructed by collecting Vietnamese SMS messages from multiple heterogeneous sources rather than relying on a single collection channel. This strategy aims to improve linguistic diversity while reducing potential sampling bias.
[45] Fig. 2. SMS Data Collection Pipeline
[46] As illustrated in Fig. 2, SMS messages were collected from four primary channels: community contributions, volunteer submissions, publicly available online resources, and partner organizations. In the dataset schema (Table 3), these sources are mapped into three standardized categorical attributes: crawl (online resources), user_report (community and volunteer submissions), and whitelist (partner organizations). Community contributions and volunteer submissions provide real-world messages received by mobile users in their daily lives, allowing the dataset to capture contemporary phishing campaigns, promotional advertisements, financial notifications, and legitimate personal communications.
[47] To protect user privacy, all collected messages underwent an anonymization process before annotation. Personally identifiable information (PII), including phone numbers, personal names, identification numbers, bank account information, URLs, and other sensitive content, was either removed or replaced with standardized placeholders while preserving the semantic characteristics required for annotation and subsequent machine learning applications.
[48] To ensure high data quality and eliminate potential noise before the formal annotation phase, a rigorous screening pipeline was executed combining automated scripts and a systematic manual inspection protocol. Out of the 2,058 raw messages initially collected from the four heterogeneous sources, a total of 51 records (2.48%) were systematically excluded based strictly on the predefined technical and semantic filtering criteria outlined in Table 1. First, an automated Python script was utilized to perform exact deduplication, removing 23 redundant message variants generated by mass-broadcast spam campaigns. Second, a structured, two-pass manual verification was conducted by the core research team to eliminate 9 messages with severe structural corruption (e.g., truncated text or unresolvable encoding failures) and 19 messages holding insufficient semantic information following the PII anonymization phase. This hybrid screening protocol ensures that the subsequent multi-annotator evaluation pipeline operates exclusively on a clean, robust, and linguistically viable corpus, reinforcing the reproducibility of our methodology.

[58] Annotation Guideline
[59] To minimize annotation subjectivity and improve labeling consistency, a standardized Annotation Guideline was developed before the annotation process. The guideline establishes a unified decision-making framework by defining annotation objectives, label definitions, decision rules, and representative examples. All annotators were required to study the guideline before participating in the annotation task.
[60] The guideline categorizes SMS messages into two primary classes: Ham and Spam. Ham messages refer to legitimate communications, including personal conversations, critical transactional notifications, official authentication messages, and service-related announcements that do not attempt to deceive or manipulate recipients.
[61] Spam is defined as a broad category comprising two semantic sub-categories: Commercial Spam, which includes unsolicited marketing promotions and unauthorized advertisements, and Fraudulent/Phishing Spam, which includes impersonation attacks, fraudulent financial schemes, and malicious messages intended to steal information or financial assets.
[62] For the baseline binary classification task, both sub-categories are assigned the same Spam label. This design reflects the objective of constructing a binary SMS spam detection dataset while preserving finer semantic distinctions in the annotation guideline for future analyses and potential taxonomy extension.
[63] The guideline also specifies decision rules for ambiguous cases. Annotators were instructed to evaluate each message according to its communication intent, contextual meaning, and potential security risk rather than isolated keywords. For example, the presence of terms such as promotion, bank, or reward does not automatically indicate spam. Instead, annotators considered whether the overall message exhibited deception, impersonation, coercion, or unauthorized commercial solicitation.
[64] Representative examples and common annotation pitfalls identified during preliminary annotation rounds were incorporated into the guideline to improve inter-annotator consistency. During the verification stage, the guideline was revised whenever systematic ambiguities were identified through disagreement analysis, supporting continuous improvement of annotation quality.
[65] Independent Multi-Annotator Labeling
[66] After completing the annotation preparation stage, the collected SMS messages were independently annotated by a team of trained annotators following the standardized Annotation Guideline described in the previous section. Independent annotation was adopted to minimize potential bias arising from communication among annotators and to ensure that each labeling decision reflected the annotator's own interpretation of the predefined annotation criteria.
[67] Each SMS message was assigned to ten independent annotators, all of whom evaluated the message without access to the labels assigned by other annotators. This independent annotation strategy provides multiple judgments for each message, allowing the subsequent verification process to evaluate annotation consistency and identify disagreement cases objectively.
[68] The annotation team comprised 10 native Vietnamese speakers (undergraduate and graduate researchers in Computer Science and Cybersecurity). All annotators completed a formal training session on the Annotation Guideline prior to labeling. Annotators were compensated fairly for their effort and signed privacy non-disclosure agreements to protect user data.
[69] Unlike majority-voting approaches that determine the final label immediately after annotation, the proposed framework intentionally separates annotation from verification. The objective of this design is to preserve all individual annotation decisions for subsequent quality assessment rather than discarding minority opinions. Consequently, every annotation record, including disagreement cases, becomes valuable evidence for evaluating annotation consistency and identifying potential weaknesses in the annotation process.
[70] After all annotators completed their labeling tasks, the annotation results were merged into a unified annotation repository. At this stage, no annotation was manually modified or removed. Instead, the merged annotation results served as the input for the dual-stage verification framework presented in the following section.
[71] Consensus Assessment and Disagreement Resolution
[72] Following independent annotation, results are subjected to a structured consensus assessment to evaluate consistency. Unlike conventional workflows that rely on majority voting, the proposed framework preserves every individual decision and systematically examines disagreement cases to identify quality issues.
[73] Messages achieving complete consensus are directly accepted into the final dataset. Conversely, disagreement cases are transferred to a dedicated analysis process. Inspired by Oortwijn et al. [7], this analysis evaluates multiple perspectives—including guideline ambiguity, conceptual inconsistency, variable annotation behavior, and human error to address root causes systematically rather than case-by-case.
[74] Ethics Statement and Data Licensing
[75] Ethics Statement: All SMS messages were collected with user consent or obtained from public reporting platforms. Sensitive personal details—including phone numbers, personal names, account credentials, and specific URLs—were systematically replaced with standardized placeholders (e.g., [PHONE], [URL], [NAME]) during anonymization to safeguard user privacy.

[165] Inter-Annotator Agreement
[166] Following the first-stage annotation process, each SMS message was independently labeled by ten annotators according to the Annotation Guideline described in Section 2. To evaluate annotation consistency, the individual annotation files were merged by SMS identifier, allowing the labels assigned by different annotators to be aligned and compared on a message-by-message basis.
[167] The merged annotation matrix was evaluated using Fleiss' Kappa, a statistical measure designed to assess agreement among multiple annotators assigning categorical labels to the same set of instances [8, 10]. Unlike Cohen's Kappa, which is mathematically restricted to a fixed pair of exactly two raters [5, 9], Cohen's framework is fundamentally unsuitable for this study because each SMS message was independently annotated by a larger group of ten annotators. Consequently, Fleiss' Kappa is uniquely appropriate as it accommodates an arbitrary number of raters per item and estimates the degree to which the observed consensus exceeds the chance-expected agreement [8].

Rather than treating these contested cases as statistical noise or discarding minority votes, they were transferred to a dedicated disagreement queue to analyze root causes and generate the final Gold Standard.

[273] 3.4.1 Experimental Setup and Implementation Details
[274] To establish a transparent, highly reproducible performance benchmark for Vietnamese SMS spam detection, we conducted a comparative empirical study using the finalized Gold Standard corpus. Before model training, a dedicated near-duplicate filtering stage was applied to remove template-based SMS messages that could introduce train-test leakage. The resulting evaluation corpus contained 1,541 unique SMS messages. The classification workflow evaluates two distinct families of algorithms: traditional machine learning architectures utilizing surface-level features and a fine-tuned deep contextual Transformer model [11].
[275] For traditional machine learning baselines, including Support Vector Machine with Linear and RBF kernels, Logistic Regression, Multinomial Naive Bayes, and a Multi-Layer Perceptron, textual representations were extracted using Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency vectorization. The feature space was configured with an n-gram range of (1, 2) and capped at a maximum vocabulary size of 5,000 features. Prior to feature extraction, Vietnamese compound words were segmented using the PyVi toolkit (ViTokenizer.tokenize). However, because PyVi relies on standard dictionary lookup, its segmentation efficacy degrades on unaccented SMS text and informal teencode (e.g., 'ck' for 'chuyển khoản', 'ngkhac' for 'người khác'), introducing tokenization noise into traditional surface feature representations.
[276] To fulfill our promise of evaluating deep pre-trained language models, we integrated vinai/phobert-base (PhoBERT-base), a state-of-the-art pre-trained Transformer architecture optimized for Vietnamese natural language processing. Fine-tuning of PhoBERT-base was executed on a dedicated workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU running Python 3.10 and PyTorch 2.2.0+cu121. The model was trained for 4 epochs using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 0.00002 (2e-5), a weight decay of 0.01, a warmup ratio of 0.1, a per-device batch size of 16, and a maximum sequence length of 128 tokens. Model selection was governed by evaluating the highest F1-score on the positive class at each epoch (load_best_model_at_end=True).
[277] 3.4.2 Near-Duplicate Filtering and Train-Test Leakage Protection
[278] To eliminate potential evaluation bias caused by near-duplicate spam campaigns, a rigorous multi-stage deduplication pipeline was executed on the 2,007 gold-standard messages prior to any dataset splitting. Phishing campaigns typically generate vast amounts of template-based messages that differ only in dynamic entities (e.g., recipient names, phone numbers, or fraudulent URLs). Performing cross-validation directly on raw data would risk placing near-identical variants into both training and testing folds, leading to artificially inflated performance metrics.
[279] To prevent this, we employed a character-level 3-gram Jaccard similarity metric. Texts were first normalized (lowercased and whitespace-trimmed) and decomposed into sets of contiguous 3-character sequences. The Jaccard index between two message sets  and  was computed as :

[281] Messages exhibiting a similarity threshold of J(T1, T2) >= 0.8 were identified as campaign variants and filtered out. This stage successfully removed 466 near-duplicate records (comprising 408 transactional Ham templates and 58 phishing Spam variants), yielding a strictly unique benchmark corpus of 1,541 messages (878 Ham and 663 Spam). This higher proportion of Ham duplicates is attributed to automated banking alerts and routine OTP confirmations that repeat identical structural templates across large user populations. This workflow guarantees that no message pair with J >= 0.8 spans across the training and evaluation splits of any fold.

[383] Among traditional machine learning baselines, SVM with RBF kernel achieved the highest overall recall (98.04% ± 0.60%) with an F1-Score of 96.46% ± 1.75%, closely followed by Linear SVM (96.22% ± 2.10%). Logistic Regression and MLP demonstrated stable convergence around ~95.76% F1-score. Conversely, Multinomial Naive Bayes registered the lowest performance (92.38% ± 1.28% F1-score) due to a noticeable degradation in recall (90.65% ± 2.71%). This performance drop stems from two primary factors: (i) the strong conditional independence assumption failing to model complex word dependencies in multi-keyword scam messages, and (ii) static TF-IDF feature spaces failing to generalize across misspelled teencode variations. In contrast, PhoBERT-base leverages subword Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenization and deep contextual attention, enabling it to map noisy teencode variants to their underlying semantic representations effectively.
[384] 3.5 Limitations
[385] While the proposed framework establishes a transparent, quality-assured dataset for Vietnamese SMS classification, several research limitations should be acknowledged:
[386] 1. In-Distribution Evaluation Scope: Model performance metrics evaluate five-fold cross-validation on an in-distribution, deduplicated benchmark corpus. Real-world performance on out-of-distribution (OOD) traffic or newly emerging zero-day phishing campaigns employing novel lexical obfuscation tactics may vary.
[387] 2. Dataset Scale and Longitudinal Expansion: The dataset currently encapsulates 2,007 gold-standard messages (1,541 unique records). Although sufficient for establishing robust baseline classifiers, continuous longitudinal collection is necessary to track evolving mobile threats.
[388] 3. Morphological Segmentation Bottlenecks: Standard Vietnamese segmenters (e.g., PyVi) exhibit reduced accuracy on non-diacritic SMS text and informal teencode, emphasizing the necessity of domain-adapted subword language models.
[389] Conclusion and Future work
[390] This study establishes a rigorous and transparent annotation methodology for constructing a high-quality Vietnamese SMS dataset, shifting the emphasis from simple data accumulation to reproducible quality control. Rather than dismissing human disagreement as statistical noise, the proposed framework leverages annotator divergence as a critical linguistic signal to uncover semantic ambiguities and systematically refine the underlying gold standard labels.
[391] The empirical analysis of the initial corpus phase demonstrates the structural efficiency of this framework. Prior to formal adjudication, the independent labeling phase involving ten annotators achieved an overall Fleiss' Kappa coefficient of 0.9856, signifying an 'Almost Perfect Agreement.' This high consensus confirms that the standardized annotation guidelines effectively established clear decision boundaries for the vast majority of messages. Human disagreement was strictly confined to a minor 2.19% subset of edge cases, primarily caused by non-standard banking notification templates and localized teencode abbreviations. By routing these specific borderline instances through the structured disagreement resolution workflow, the methodology effectively guarantees the integrity of the finalized Gold Standard dataset.
[392] At the current developmental stage, the corpus comprises 2,058 collected messages, of which 2,007 have successfully progressed through the complete multi-annotator validation pipeline. To ensure absolute transparency and facilitate open-science collaboration, the final benchmark dataset is packaged alongside its rigorous Annotation Guideline, granular disagreement metadata, and diverse distribution formats.
[393] Future initiatives will actively expand this corpus beyond its current phase to achieve a targeted milestone of over 5,000 annotated Vietnamese SMS messages. This expansion will specifically focus on capturing newly emerging, sophisticated phishing variants to continuously stress-test and evolve the proposed resolution framework. Ultimately, this scaling corpus will serve as a foundational, high-fidelity benchmark for training and evaluating next-generation intelligent cybersecurity detection systems within the Vietnamese natural language processing domain.
